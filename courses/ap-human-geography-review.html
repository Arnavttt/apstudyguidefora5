Exam Day Tips

Know all major geographic models: Demographic Transition, Von Thünen, Christaller, Rostow, Wallerstein.

✓

✓

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. The demographic transition model's second stage is characterized by:

  • A. High birth rates and high death rates
  • B. High birth rates and falling death rates, producing rapid population growth
  • C. Low birth rates and low death rates
  • D. Falling birth rates and low death rates
Show Answer

Answer: B — Stage 2: improved sanitation, medicine, and food security reduce death rates while birth rates remain high, causing rapid natural increase.

Q2. Ravenstein's Laws of Migration suggest most migrants:

  • A. Move internationally
  • B. Move short distances and in steps toward urban centers
  • C. Flee political persecution
  • D. Move permanently and never return
Show Answer

Answer: B — Ravenstein's 19th-century laws: most migrants move short distances, migration proceeds step-by-step toward major cities, each stream produces a counter-stream.

Q3. 'Friction of distance' refers to:

  • A. Mountain barriers preventing migration
  • B. The decrease in interaction between places as distance between them increases
  • C. Difficulty of building roads in rough terrain
  • D. Border controls reducing migration
Show Answer

Answer: B — Friction of distance: the farther apart two places are, the less interaction occurs between them—a fundamental principle underlying gravity models.

Q4. Fordism as an economic system is characterized by:

  • A. Flexible post-industrial production
  • B. Mass production of standardized goods using assembly-line methods, producing large quantities at low unit costs
  • C. Artisanal craft production
  • D. Agglomeration of high-tech industries
Show Answer

Answer: B — Fordism involves standardized mass production, vertical integration, and mass consumption—the dominant industrial model of the early-to-mid 20th century.

Q5. The 'rank-size rule' predicts that:

  • A. All cities in a country are equal in population
  • B. The nth-largest city has 1/n the population of the largest city
  • C. Capital cities are always the largest
  • D. Coastal cities always dominate inland ones
Show Answer

Answer: B — The rank-size rule: if cities are ranked by population, the second city has 1/2 the largest city's population, the third city 1/3, etc.

Q6. Thomas Malthus's population theory predicted that:

  • A. Technological innovation would outpace population growth
  • B. Population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, inevitably leading to famine or war
  • C. Migration would solve overpopulation
  • D. Industrialization would reduce birth rates
Show Answer

Answer: B — Malthus argued unchecked population grows exponentially while food production grows linearly, so 'positive checks' (famine, disease, war) inevitably limit population.

Q7. The UN's Human Development Index (HDI) measures:

  • A. GDP per capita alone
  • B. A composite of life expectancy, education, and income per capita
  • C. Military strength and territorial size
  • D. Environmental sustainability
Show Answer

Answer: B — HDI combines three dimensions: health (life expectancy), education (mean/expected years of schooling), and standard of living (GNI per capita).

Q8. Bid-rent theory explains urban land use patterns by arguing that:

  • A. Governments zone land for specific uses
  • B. Land use is determined by how much different users will pay for proximity to the city center
  • C. All urban land has equal value
  • D. Industrial zones always locate near rivers
Show Answer

Answer: B — Bid-rent theory: different users (retailers, offices, residents) have different rent gradients based on distance from CBD, producing concentric zone patterns.

Q9. The 'epidemiological transition' describes:

  • A. International spread of disease through trade
  • B. A shift from infectious/parasitic diseases to degenerative/chronic diseases as societies develop
  • C. Vaccination programs eliminating childhood diseases
  • D. Climate change affecting disease distribution
Show Answer

Answer: B — Omran's epidemiological transition: pre-modern societies die from epidemics and famine; modern societies face cardiovascular disease, cancer, and chronic conditions.

Q10. 'Diaspora' in human geography refers to:

  • A. The movement of animals across continents
  • B. The dispersion of a people from their original homeland while maintaining cultural identity and connections to it
  • C. The diffusion of languages through trade
  • D. Refugees seeking asylum
Show Answer

Answer: B — Diaspora: communities living outside their ancestral homeland while maintaining cultural, social, and sometimes political ties to it.

Q11. Centripetal forces in a state are those that:

  • A. Cause ethnic fragmentation
  • B. Unify the state and bind citizens together (common language, national identity, shared history, stable institutions)
  • C. Promote regional autonomy
  • D. Cause migration to cities
Show Answer

Answer: B — Centripetal forces promote national cohesion: shared language, religion, strong national identity, effective governance.

Q12. 'Placelessness' (Edward Relph) describes:

  • A. Rural areas lacking urban amenities
  • B. The homogenization of landscapes through globalization, making places indistinguishable from one another
  • C. Areas without cultural identity
  • D. Urban sprawl
Show Answer

Answer: B — Relph's placelessness: global capitalism and mass culture produce landscapes of similarity—McDonald's, Walmart, identical malls—stripping places of distinctive character.

Q13. Wallerstein's World-Systems Theory classifies countries as:

  • A. Developed, developing, and underdeveloped
  • B. Core, semi-periphery, and periphery based on their role in global capitalist production
  • C. Democratic and authoritarian
  • D. Urban and rural
Show Answer

Answer: B — Core nations (US, Germany, Japan) control capital and high-value production; periphery nations supply raw materials; semi-periphery (Brazil, China) occupies intermediate roles.

Q14. The 'gravity model' predicts that interaction between two places is:

  • A. Random and unpredictable
  • B. Proportional to their population sizes and inversely proportional to the distance between them
  • C. Determined by cultural similarities
  • D. Controlled by government policy
Show Answer

Answer: B — The gravity model: interaction = (Pop1 x Pop2) / Distance squared. Larger and closer places interact more.

Q15. 'Sequent occupance' refers to:

  • A. The sequential expansion of empires
  • B. The succession of distinct human groups occupying a place over time, each leaving cultural imprints on the landscape
  • C. The order in which settlers claim land
  • D. Agricultural succession of crops
Show Answer

Answer: B — Sequent occupance (Whittlesey): landscapes reflect layers of successive occupation—e.g., a New England town shows Indigenous, colonial, industrial, and post-industrial elements.

Q16. The 'second agricultural revolution' (17th-19th century Europe) was characterized by:

  • A. Irrigation and river valley farming
  • B. Crop rotation, selective breeding, new tools, and enclosure increasing agricultural productivity
  • C. Introduction of New World crops to Europe
  • D. Industrial-scale fertilizer production
Show Answer

Answer: B — New rotations, drainage, selective breeding, and enclosure dramatically increased yields and released agricultural labor for urban factories.

Q17. Devolution in political geography refers to:

  • A. Countries expanding their territory
  • B. The transfer of power from central governments to regional sub-units in response to regional political pressures
  • C. The collapse of nation-states
  • D. Migration of populations toward city centers
Show Answer

Answer: B — Devolution: pressures from regional movements (Scotland in UK, Catalonia in Spain) lead central governments to transfer legislative/administrative powers to regional governments.

Q18. The 'green revolution' of the 1960s-70s primarily benefited which type of farmer?

  • A. Subsistence farmers with small plots
  • B. Large-scale commercial farmers able to purchase fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation infrastructure
  • C. Organic farmers
  • D. Nomadic pastoralists
Show Answer

Answer: B — The Green Revolution's high-yield varieties required purchased inputs. Wealthier farmers could afford these; poorer farmers without capital were often marginalized.

Q19. Urban primacy describes a situation where:

  • A. A country has many large cities of similar size
  • B. One city (usually the capital) contains a disproportionate share of the country's urban population, economic activity, and political power
  • C. All cities develop at equal rates
  • D. Cities are evenly distributed geographically
Show Answer

Answer: B — Urban primacy: Bangkok contains ~40% of Thailand's urban population—far exceeding what rank-size rule would predict. Reflects colonial patterns and political centralization.

Q20. The 'cornucopian' position in population-resources debates holds that:

  • A. Population growth always causes resource depletion
  • B. Human ingenuity and technological innovation will continuously find solutions to resource scarcity
  • C. Environmental sustainability requires population control
  • D. Resources are infinite
Show Answer

Answer: B — Cornucopians (Julian Simon) argue that prices signal scarcity, incentivizing innovation and substitution—history shows resource availability often improves as technology advances.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

Refer to the demographic transition model. a) Describe the population characteristics of a country in Stage 2 of the demographic transition. b) Explain TWO reasons why birth rates eventually decline as countries move from Stage 2 to Stage 3. c) Explain ONE challenge a country in Stage 4 or 5 (aging population) faces.
View Model Answer
a) A country in Stage 2 has high birth rates (35-45 per 1,000) combined with rapidly falling death rates (declining from 30-40 to 15-20 per 1,000 as sanitation, medicine, and food security improve). This produces rapid natural increase—population growth rates of 2-3% annually. Age structure pyramids show wide bases (large youth cohorts) and narrow tops. Life expectancy rises significantly during this stage—from 35-40 years to 50-65 years. Examples include 19th-century Europe and many contemporary sub-Saharan African nations. b) Reason 1 — Urbanization and opportunity cost of children: As countries urbanize and industrialize, children shift from economic assets (farm labor) to economic costs (requiring education, consuming urban housing space). Urban women gain access to education and labor market opportunities, raising the opportunity cost of childbearing. Female education is the single strongest predictor of fertility decline. Reason 2 — Improved child survival: In Stage 2, parents have many children partly as insurance against child mortality. As child mortality falls, parents need fewer births to achieve desired family size. This 'child survival hypothesis' explains the delayed fertility decline: parents wait for evidence that children reliably survive before reducing births. c) A Stage 4/5 country (Japan, Italy, Germany) faces severe demographic aging: a large elderly population drawing pensions and healthcare while a shrinking working-age population supports them. The dependency ratio rises. Japan spends ~10% of GDP on pension and healthcare costs for the elderly. Solutions are painful: raise retirement age, increase immigration (politically controversial), or reduce benefits. Demographic momentum makes rapid reversal nearly impossible even with pro-natalist policies.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

a) Define the concept of 'sense of place' and explain its geographic significance. b) Using a specific example, explain how globalization has challenged local sense of place. c) Explain ONE way communities have responded to preserve local identity against global homogenization.
View Model Answer
a) 'Sense of place' refers to the subjective emotional and cultural meanings that people attach to specific locations—the feelings, memories, and identity investments that make a place feel meaningful and distinct. Geographers like Yi-Fu Tuan (topophilia) and Edward Relph distinguish between 'place' (humanly meaningful location) and 'space' (abstract geometric location). Sense of place is geographically significant because it shapes behavior (people fight to preserve beloved places), identity (places form individual and community identity), and landscape character. b) The global spread of fast food chains illustrates how globalization challenges local sense of place. When McDonald's, KFC, and Starbucks open in historic city centers—as they have in Rome's Piazza di Spagna and Beijing's Wangfujing—they introduce standardized global commercial aesthetics that compete with local establishments. Small trattorias and tea houses lose business to global chains offering predictability. The landscape becomes what Relph calls 'placeless'—similar to any other global city rather than distinctively Roman or Chinese. c) The Slow Food Movement (founded in Bra, Italy, 1989) represents a community response to preserve local culinary identity against fast-food homogenization. The movement valorizes local ingredients, traditional recipes, and artisanal production. It has established 'Ark of Taste' catalogs documenting traditional foods at risk of extinction and created Presidia projects supporting traditional producers. By making local food identity a source of pride and economic value, it creates incentives for maintaining place-specific food cultures against competitive pressure of global standardization.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

a) Explain the difference between a nation and a state in political geography. b) Explain why multinational states face centrifugal pressures using ONE specific example. c) Explain ONE strategy states use to build national identity (nation-building).
View Model Answer
a) In political geography, a 'state' is a political unit with defined territory, permanent population, effective government, and recognized sovereignty—what everyday language calls a 'country' (e.g., France, Nigeria, Brazil). A 'nation' is a cultural community defined by shared language, religion, history, or identity whose members feel collective belonging and aspire to self-governance (e.g., the Kurdish nation, the Catalan nation). Most states in the world are multinational states. Conversely, stateless nations lack their own sovereign political unit. b) Nigeria illustrates multinational state centrifugal pressures. Nigeria contains approximately 250 ethnic groups, most significantly the Hausa-Fulani (Muslim, north), Yoruba (southwest), and Igbo (southeast). The Biafran War (1967-70) was a catastrophic attempt at Igbo secession. Contemporary Boko Haram insurgency reflects northern Muslim alienation. Oil revenue allocation creates perpetual north-south tensions. Nigeria's federal structure attempts to manage these centrifugal forces, but the pressures remain intense. c) Education is a primary nation-building strategy. States use standardized national curricula, required national history instruction, and official language education to inculcate shared national identity in children. France's 19th-century strategy of requiring French instruction in all schools ('turning peasants into Frenchmen') suppressed regional languages (Breton, Occitan) while building unified French national consciousness. Textbooks emphasize shared national myths, heroes, and historical narratives. Pledge of allegiance ceremonies and national holiday celebrations reinforce national identity ritualistically.
