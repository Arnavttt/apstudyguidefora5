Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. Pavlov's classical conditioning experiments demonstrated that:

  • A. Organisms learn through reward and punishment
  • B. A neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus can come to elicit a conditioned response
  • C. The unconscious mind drives behavior
  • D. Cognitive schemas determine learning
Show Answer

Answer: B — Pavlov showed that a bell (neutral stimulus) repeatedly paired with food (UCS) eventually elicited salivation (CR) on its own—establishing the principles of classical conditioning.

Q2. Erikson's primary developmental challenge of adolescence is:

  • A. Trust vs. mistrust
  • B. Industry vs. inferiority
  • C. Identity vs. role confusion
  • D. Intimacy vs. isolation
Show Answer

Answer: C — Erikson's fifth stage (adolescence, 12-18): the central challenge is forming a coherent identity integrating multiple roles, values, and experiences into a stable self-concept.

Q3. The 'mere exposure effect' refers to:

  • A. Advertising creating desire for products
  • B. The tendency to develop greater liking for stimuli simply through repeated exposure
  • C. Conformity to social norms
  • D. Obedience to authority
Show Answer

Answer: B — Zajonc's mere exposure effect: familiarity breeds liking. Repeated exposure to a stimulus increases positive evaluation, even without conscious awareness.

Q4. Freud's id, ego, and superego represent:

  • A. Conscious, preconscious, and unconscious mind
  • B. Three agencies representing instinctual drives, reality-mediating self, and internalized moral standards
  • C. Three stages of psychosexual development
  • D. Defense mechanisms
Show Answer

Answer: B — Freud's structural model: the id (pleasure principle), ego (reality principle), and superego (internalized moral standards) are three psychic agencies in constant dynamic tension.

Q5. In Milgram's obedience experiments, the majority of participants:

  • A. Refused to administer shocks above mild levels
  • B. Continued administering apparently dangerous shocks when commanded by the experimenter
  • C. Spontaneously helped the learner escape
  • D. Correctly predicted others would obey but themselves refused
Show Answer

Answer: B — Milgram's finding: ~65% of participants administered maximum (450V) apparent shocks when commanded by an authority figure—demonstrating situational power over moral behavior.

Q6. 'Confirmation bias' describes the tendency to:

  • A. Adjust beliefs based on new evidence
  • B. Seek, interpret, and remember information in ways that confirm existing beliefs
  • C. Overestimate the prevalence of vivid events
  • D. Make systematic attributional errors
Show Answer

Answer: B — Confirmation bias: people preferentially attend to, remember, and interpret evidence supporting their existing views, making belief revision surprisingly difficult.

Q7. Piaget's concept of 'object permanence' refers to:

  • A. The ability to classify objects by size
  • B. The understanding that objects continue to exist when not directly perceived
  • C. Conservation of matter
  • D. Reversible logical operations
Show Answer

Answer: B — Object permanence develops around 8-12 months in Piaget's sensorimotor stage: before this, infants behave as if objects cease to exist when out of sight.

Q8. The 'bystander effect' predicts that as the number of observers increases:

  • A. Each person becomes more likely to help
  • B. Each person becomes less likely to intervene, due to diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance
  • C. Social cohesion increases
  • D. Helping behavior is more rapid
Show Answer

Answer: B — Darley and Latané: with more bystanders, each individual's felt responsibility diminishes (diffusion of responsibility), and each looks to others for cues (pluralistic ignorance).

Q9. Biological psychology examines psychological phenomena by:

  • A. Focusing exclusively on early childhood experiences
  • B. Investigating the role of genes, brain structures, neurotransmitters, and hormones in behavior and mental processes
  • C. Studying environmental determinants of behavior
  • D. Analyzing cultural differences in cognition
Show Answer

Answer: B — Biological/neuroscience approach: behavior and mental processes emerge from biological mechanisms—brain structure, neurotransmitters (serotonin and depression), genetics, and evolutionary adaptations.

Q10. The 'fundamental attribution error' describes:

  • A. Correctly identifying situational causes of behavior
  • B. Underestimating situational factors and overestimating dispositional factors when explaining others' behavior
  • C. Taking credit for successes and blaming situations for failures
  • D. Attributing group behavior to individual members
Show Answer

Answer: B — FAE: when explaining others' behavior, we systematically underweight situational causes and overweight dispositional ones—a pervasive error in social judgment.

Q11. Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy holds that depression results primarily from:

  • A. Unconscious conflicts about sexuality
  • B. Automatic negative thoughts forming a cognitive triad of negative views of self, world, and future
  • C. Learned helplessness from uncontrollable shocks
  • D. Serotonin deficiency alone
Show Answer

Answer: B — Beck's cognitive model: depression is maintained by a cognitive triad—automatic negative thoughts about oneself, the world, and the future—which cognitive therapy targets.

Q12. 'Neuroplasticity' refers to:

  • A. The fixed, immutable structure of the adult brain
  • B. The brain's ability to reorganize by forming new neural connections in response to experience or injury
  • C. The speed of neural impulse transmission
  • D. Genetic determination of brain structure
Show Answer

Answer: B — Neuroplasticity: the brain retains the ability to modify its structure and function throughout life—challenging older 'fixed adult brain' views.

Q13. In operant conditioning, 'negative reinforcement' involves:

  • A. Punishment that decreases behavior
  • B. Removing an aversive stimulus following a behavior, which increases that behavior's frequency
  • C. Adding an unpleasant stimulus to decrease behavior
  • D. Withdrawal of reward to extinguish behavior
Show Answer

Answer: B — Negative reinforcement strengthens behavior by removing something aversive—taking ibuprofen removes headache pain (reinforcing pill-taking). Distinct from punishment.

Q14. The DSM-5's categorical approach to mental disorders has been criticized primarily because:

  • A. It includes too few disorders
  • B. Psychological symptoms exist on continua rather than discrete categories, and comorbidity is extremely common
  • C. It ignores biological factors
  • D. Diagnoses are too difficult for clinicians to use
Show Answer

Answer: B — Categorical diagnosis assumes disorders are qualitatively distinct, but psychological symptoms show continuous distributions and highly comorbid patterns inconsistent with discrete disease entities.

Q15. Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs has which level at the base?

  • A. Love and belonging
  • B. Self-esteem
  • C. Physiological needs
  • D. Safety needs
Show Answer

Answer: C — Maslow's hierarchy from base to apex: physiological (food, water, sleep) -> safety -> love/belonging -> esteem -> self-actualization.

Q16. Twin studies demonstrate regarding the heritability of intelligence:

  • A. IQ is entirely genetic
  • B. Both genetic and environmental factors contribute, with heritability estimates typically 50-80% in adulthood
  • C. IQ is entirely determined by education
  • D. There are no twin studies supporting genetic contributions
Show Answer

Answer: B — Twin studies show substantial genetic influence on IQ, with heritability increasing from ~40% in childhood to ~60-80% in adulthood. Both genes and environment contribute.

Q17. 'Proactive interference' in memory refers to:

  • A. New learning interfering with recall of old memories
  • B. Old learning interfering with recall of new information
  • C. Repression of traumatic memories
  • D. The tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon
Show Answer

Answer: B — Proactive interference: previously learned material interferes with learning and recall of new material—e.g., knowing Spanish interferes with learning Italian vocabulary.

Q18. The 'halo effect' in social cognition describes:

  • A. The influence of attractive people on jury decisions
  • B. The tendency for one positive characteristic to influence overall positive judgments of a person
  • C. The impact of first impressions on subsequent evaluations
  • D. Confirmation bias in social settings
Show Answer

Answer: B — The halo effect: one salient positive attribute (physical attractiveness, prestige) creates a generalized positive impression, leading to overestimation of other unrelated positive qualities.

Q19. Which neurotransmitter is most associated with reward and the reinforcing effects of addictive substances?

  • A. Serotonin
  • B. Dopamine
  • C. GABA
  • D. Acetylcholine
Show Answer

Answer: B — Dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway (nucleus accumbens) mediates reward prediction and reinforcement. Addictive substances all increase dopaminergic activity.

Q20. 'Self-serving bias' in attribution theory refers to:

  • A. Taking credit for others' successes
  • B. Attributing personal successes to internal factors and failures to external factors, protecting self-esteem
  • C. Overestimating one's abilities relative to others
  • D. Judging others by the same standards as oneself
Show Answer

Answer: B — Self-serving bias: success -> 'I'm talented' (internal); failure -> 'The test was unfair' (external). This asymmetric attribution style protects self-esteem.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

A researcher is studying whether listening to classical music while studying improves long-term retention. a) Describe how the researcher could design a true experiment to test this hypothesis. b) Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and one potential confounding variable. c) Explain why the researcher should use random assignment and what threat to validity it addresses.
View Model Answer
a) Experimental Design: The researcher randomly assigns 60 college students to two conditions. The experimental group studies a 2,000-word passage while listening to Mozart's Symphony No. 40 at a standard volume. The control group studies the identical passage in silence. All other conditions are held constant: same room, same lighting, same 30-minute study period, same time of day. One week later, both groups complete an identical 30-question recall test. The researcher compares mean test scores between conditions using an independent-samples t-test. b) Independent variable: Whether classical music is playing during study (music vs. no music—the manipulated condition). Dependent variable: Score on the recall test administered one week after studying (the measured outcome). Confounding variable: Prior musical training of participants. Students with music training may find background music less distracting, while untrained students might find it more distracting. If musical training is unevenly distributed between groups, observed differences could be due to this variable rather than the music itself. c) Random assignment—using a random process (coin flip, random number generator) to assign participants to conditions—addresses the threat of selection bias to internal validity. Without random assignment, observed differences might reflect pre-existing differences between participants. Random assignment distributes individual differences (intelligence, study skills, musical background, motivation) approximately equally between conditions, so group differences in the dependent variable can more plausibly be attributed to the independent variable. This is why the true experiment is the gold standard for establishing causation.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

Apply psychological concepts to the following scenario: Mia, age 16, has experienced persistent sadness, loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities, difficulty concentrating, and sleeping 12+ hours daily for 6 weeks. She has withdrawn from friends and expressed feelings of worthlessness. a) Identify the most likely psychological disorder and explain TWO symptoms supporting this identification. b) Describe ONE biological and ONE cognitive explanation for Mia's condition. c) Describe ONE biological and ONE psychological treatment approach.
View Model Answer
a) Mia is most likely experiencing Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). The DSM-5 requires at least five depressive symptoms present for two or more consecutive weeks, including depressed mood or anhedonia. Symptom 1: Anhedonia (loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities). Mia's withdrawal from previously pleasurable activities is a cardinal symptom of MDD, reflecting disruption of the brain's reward system. Symptom 2: Hypersomnia (sleeping 12+ hours daily). Significantly increased sleep is a neurovegetative symptom of MDD, reflecting dysregulation of sleep-wake cycles. Combined with her six-week duration and additional symptoms (concentration difficulties, worthlessness, social withdrawal), these meet DSM-5 MDD criteria. b) Biological explanation: Monoamine deficiency hypothesis—MDD is associated with reduced availability of monoamine neurotransmitters, particularly serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine. Serotonin dysregulation affects mood regulation; dopamine deficits in mesolimbic pathways produce anhedonia. Structural differences including reduced hippocampal volume (from stress hormone cortisol effects) are also observed. Cognitive explanation: Beck's cognitive triad. Mia's feelings of worthlessness reflect automatic negative cognitions about herself ('I'm worthless, unlovable'). These negative self-schemas were likely formed through early experiences and are now activated, causing her to interpret ambiguous events negatively and selectively attend to negative information. c) Biological treatment: Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine (Prozac) or sertraline (Zoloft) are first-line pharmacological treatment for adolescent MDD. SSRIs block reuptake of serotonin, increasing its availability, typically producing mood improvement after 2-4 weeks of consistent use. Psychological treatment: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) targeting Beck's cognitive triad. A therapist helps Mia identify automatic negative thoughts, evaluate evidence for and against these thoughts, and develop more accurate and adaptive cognitive patterns. Behavioral activation—scheduling rewarding activities to counteract withdrawal—addresses the anhedonia and social isolation that maintain depression.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

a) Explain the difference between classical and operant conditioning using a specific example for each. b) Explain how 'extinction' occurs in each type of conditioning. c) Explain ONE real-world application of operant conditioning principles.
View Model Answer
a) Classical conditioning involves learning an association between two stimuli. Example: A child repeatedly hears the dentist's drill (neutral stimulus) while experiencing pain (unconditioned stimulus producing fear). Eventually, merely hearing the drill sound (conditioned stimulus) elicits anxiety (conditioned response) even before any painful procedure begins. Operant conditioning involves learning an association between a behavior and its consequences. Example: A student who studies diligently (behavior) and receives an A (positive reinforcement—adding a pleasant stimulus) becomes more likely to study diligently for future exams. The behavior is strengthened because it was followed by a desirable consequence. b) In classical conditioning, extinction occurs when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus, causing the conditioned response to gradually weaken and disappear. The dentist's drill presented repeatedly without pain would eventually lose its ability to elicit fear. However, spontaneous recovery—temporary return of the CR after a rest period—shows the original association is suppressed, not erased. In operant conditioning, extinction occurs when a previously reinforced behavior is no longer followed by the reinforcer, causing the behavior to decrease in frequency. A rat trained to press a lever for food pellets will eventually stop pressing if the pellets stop coming. Behavioral extinction can produce an 'extinction burst' (temporary increase in responding) before the behavior fades. c) Token economies apply operant conditioning by awarding tokens (secondary reinforcers) for target behaviors (taking medications, completing assignments, appropriate social interactions). Tokens are exchanged for privileges or items (primary reinforcers). This system uses positive reinforcement, clear contingencies, and immediate feedback to shape desired behaviors. Token economies have demonstrated effectiveness in psychiatric rehabilitation, autism spectrum interventions, and classroom management.
